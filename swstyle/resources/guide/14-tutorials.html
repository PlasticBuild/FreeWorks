14. Tutorials

Each tutorial is a whole job from the first click to the last, done with the real commands and the names you see on the ribbon and in the panels. Do them in order or pick one. Every step says what to click and what you should see; if what you see is different, say so (Reporting a problem).

Tutorial 1: A static study of a bracket

You will model an L-shaped bracket, hold its base, push on its upright and read how far it bends and how safe it is. It takes about 30 minutes. It uses the parts of the add-on described in chapter 1 (sketching and features) and chapter 11 (simulation).

Part 1: the bracket

  1. Press Ctrl+N, choose Part, click OK. The status bar says "Editing Part".
  2. Click Front Plane in the feature tree, then Sketch on the pop-up toolbar.
  3. Choose Corner Rectangle on the Sketch tab. Click the origin, move up and to the right, and click. The rectangle is the base of the bracket.
  4. Choose Smart Dimension. Click the bottom edge and type 100, then click a side edge and type 10. When the sketch turns black it is fully defined.
  5. Click Exit Sketch in the confirmation corner (top right of the view).
  6. Click Extruded Boss/Base on the Features tab. Type 40 for the depth and press Enter: one Enter takes the value and finishes the panel (a click on OK does the same). The tree shows Boss-Extrude1.
  7. Click Front Plane and Sketch again. Draw a second Corner Rectangle from the origin, up the left side: dimension it 10 wide and 60 high.
  8. Exit the sketch and choose Extruded Boss/Base. Type 40 and press Enter. The two blocks join as one L-shaped body.
  9. Choose Fillet. Pick the inside corner edge where the upright meets the base, click in the Radius box, type 8 and press Enter. Without the click in the box the value goes nowhere and Enter accepts the default 10 mm.
  10. Press Ctrl+S and save the part as Bracket.

Part 2: the study

  1. On the Simulation tab click New Study and choose Static. The study is named Static 1, a tab with its name appears under the view, and the study tree appears below the feature tree.
  2. Click Apply Material. In the Material dialog choose a material, for example 6061 Alloy, and click Apply. The status bar says "Static 1: 6061 Alloy applied". Click Close.

Part 3: hold and load the bracket

  1. Click the small arrow beside Fixtures Advisor and choose Fixed Geometry (the main part of the button opens the Fixtures page of the Simulation Advisor in the Task Pane instead). Click the underside of the base, then click OK (the green check). The tree shows Fixed-1.
  2. Click the small arrow beside External Loads Advisor and choose Force; the panel that opens is titled Force/Torque. Click the outside face of the upright. Choose Normal, leave the unit and set the value to 200. If the arrows push the wrong way, tick Reverse direction. Click OK.

Part 4: mesh and run

  1. Click Create Mesh. Move the Mesh Density slider one or two steps toward Fine and click OK.
  2. Click Run This Study. The pointer shows the wait cursor while the solver works. When it ends, the status bar says "Static 1 finished." and Stress1 is shown on the model with its colour legend.

Part 5: read the results

  1. Under Results double-click Displacement1. Find the largest movement in the legend and the mark on the model.
  2. Right-click Results and choose Define Stress Plot... if you want a second stress view; set the units to N/mm^2 (MPa) so the numbers compare with the material's yield strength.
  3. Right-click Results and choose Define Factor Of Safety Plot.... In step 1 choose Max von Mises Stress, in step 2 choose Yield strength and a multiplier of 1, and in step 3 read "Lowest factor of safety". A value above 1 means the bracket stays below the yield strength under this load.
  4. Right-click Stress1 and choose Probe, choose At location, then click the fillet in the view. The value there is added to the results table.

Part 6: the report

  1. On the Simulation tab click Report. In Report Options leave all the sections ticked, type a sentence under Conclusion, and click Publish.
  2. The status bar says "Report published: <path>". The report is an HTML file beside the saved part, named like Bracket-Static 1-1, with pictures of the model and of each plot.

Try next

  • Double-click Mesh and make the mesh finer. The largest stress usually moves a little each time; when it stops moving the mesh is fine enough.
  • Double-click the load and change the value, or change the radius of the fillet, then run again and compare with Compare Results.
  • Change the material and see what the factor of safety does.

Tutorial 2: A sheet metal bracket, folded and flat

You will sketch a plate, turn it into sheet metal, fold one edge up, cut a hole, flatten the part to see the blank, fold it back, and then put the flat pattern on a drawing with its bend notes and a bend table. It takes about 40 minutes. It uses the sheet metal part of chapter 4 and chapter 6. Sheet metal needs the free SheetMetal add-on; if it is missing, the first sheet metal button offers to install it (chapter 10).

Part 1: the plate

  1. Press Ctrl+N, choose Part, click OK.
  2. Click Top Plane in the feature tree, then Sketch. Choose Corner Rectangle and draw a rectangle with one corner on the origin.
  3. Choose Smart Dimension and dimension the rectangle 100 by 60. The sketch turns black: fully defined.
  4. Click Exit Sketch.
  5. Click the sketch in the feature tree. Open the Sheet Metal tab and click Base Flange/Tab. (With no sketch picked, the panel opens with a message asking for a plane, a flat face or an existing sketch: pick the sketch then.)
  6. In the panel leave Use material sheet metal parameters and Use gauge table unticked. Check T1 (thickness) is 2.000 mm and R1 (bend radius) is 1.00 mm, Bend Allowance K-Factor with K 0.5000, and Auto Relief with Use relief ratio ticked and Ratio 0.50. Press Enter (OK).
  7. The tree gains Base-Flange1, then Sheet-Metal, a Cut list with a sheet row, and a Flat-Pattern folder at the end.

Part 2: the flange

  1. Click the top edge of the sheet on the +X side (the 60 mm edge), then Edge Flange on the Sheet Metal tab. The edge is already in the list.
  2. In the panel keep Use default radius ticked (1.00 mm) and the Angle at 90.00 deg. Under Flange Length choose Blind and type 10. Press Enter (OK). A 10 mm wall stands up on the edge, and Edge-Flange1 joins the tree.

Part 3: a hole

  1. Click the top face of the sheet, then Sketch. Draw a circle on the flat part of the sheet, well away from the flange.
  2. Choose Smart Dimension: give the circle a diameter of 10, then dimension its centre 30 from the end farthest from the flange and 30 from one long edge. The sketch turns black. Click Exit Sketch.
  3. Click Extruded Cut on the Features tab. In the panel choose Blind with a depth of 5 and tick Link to thickness, so the depth is the sheet's thickness and follows it. Press Enter (OK). A 10 mm hole goes right through the 2 mm sheet.

Part 4: flatten and fold back

  1. Click Flatten on the Sheet Metal tab. The part shows flat: one blank about 107 by 60 mm (the length is the plate plus the flange less the bend deduction, 107.14 mm for these values), with the hole and one bend line. The sheet metal features are shown greyed while the part is flat.
  2. Click Flatten again. The part folds back.
  3. Press Ctrl+S and save the part as Bracket-SM. A drawing view needs a saved part.

Part 5: the flat pattern on a drawing

  1. Press Ctrl+N, choose Drawing, and pick a sheet format, for example A3 Landscape. The Model View panel opens.
  2. Pick Bracket-SM in the list of open documents and click the arrow to go on.
  3. Under Orientation open More views and choose (A) Flat pattern. (The View Palette in the task pane also lists "(A) Flat pattern" for a sheet metal part: drag it onto the sheet.) The part does not need to be flattened first.
  4. Click the sheet to place the view. The panel says "Flat pattern placed. OK when done." Click OK. The view has its bend lines, and each bend line carries a bend note, for example "UP 90° R 1". Document Properties, Sheet Metal controls the notes, the grain direction and the bounding box.
  5. Click the flat-pattern view. On the Annotation tab open the Tables flyout and choose Bend Table, then click the sheet where the table goes. It has one row for each bend, with UP or DOWN, the angle and the radius.

Try next

  • Change the thickness of the sheet and watch the flat length, the bend notes and the table follow.
  • Add a second Edge Flange on the opposite edge and flatten again.
  • Change Bend Allowance to a different K-Factor and compare the flat length.

Tutorial 3: Three sizes of one part, from a design table

You will model a block, name its two sizes, and let a design table make three configurations (Small, Medium and Large) that you switch between. It takes about 20 minutes. It uses the configurations and design tables described in chapter 2.

Part 1: the block

  1. Press Ctrl+N, choose Part, click OK.
  2. Click Top Plane, then Sketch. Choose Corner Rectangle and draw a rectangle with one corner on the origin.
  3. Choose Smart Dimension and dimension it 100 by 50. A design table changes these sizes later, so fully define the sketch first: a corner on the origin and horizontal and vertical sides, with both dimensions in place. The sketch should be black.
  4. Click the 100 dimension. On the Value tab of the Dimension panel, type Width in the name box under Primary Value. Name the 50 dimension Height in the same way. Press Enter (OK) each time.
  5. Click Exit Sketch, then Extruded Boss/Base, type 20 and press Enter (OK). The block is 100 by 50 by 20 and the tree shows Boss-Extrude1.

Part 2: the design table

  1. Choose Insert, Tables, Excel Design Table. The panel has three groups. Source has Blank, Auto-create (leave it on), and From file with a path box and Browse.... Edit Control has "Allow model edits to update the design table" (leave it on) and "Block model edits that would update the design table". Options has New parameters and New configurations ticked, Warn when updating design table ticked and Enable cell drop-down lists unticked.
  2. Click OK. The Dimensions box asks "Select the dimensions to add to the design table:". It lists Width@Sketch1, Height@Sketch1 and the extrude's depths. Tick Width and Height and click OK.
  3. The table window opens. Row 1 reads "Design Table for: Part1", row 2 holds the two parameters, and row 3 is Default with 100 and 50. The hint under the table reads: "Type a configuration name in column A and a parameter in row 2, or right-click a row 2 cell to pick a dimension or a feature. OK updates the part."
  4. In column A, in the rows under Default, type the names, and in the same rows type the values. Double-click a cell, type, and press Enter (Enter moves down a cell, as in a spreadsheet):
ConfigurationWidthHeight
Small6030
Medium10050
Large15080
  1. Click OK to close the window. A message says "The design table made these configurations:" and lists Small, Medium and Large.

Part 3: switch between the sizes

  1. Click the ConfigurationManager tab, the third tab at the top of the left pane. It shows "Part1 Configuration(s) (Default)", then Tables, Design Table, then Default [ Part1 ], Small, Medium and Large. The feature tree also has a Design Table row.
  2. Double-click Small. The block becomes 60 by 30. Double-click Medium (100 by 50) and Large (150 by 80). The top of the ConfigurationManager names the active one. (Right-click, Show Configuration does the same.)
  3. To change the table later, right-click Design Table and choose Edit Table, or double-click the Design Table row in the feature tree.
  4. Press Ctrl+S and save the part as Block. The table is stored inside the part: you do not need a spreadsheet program to make or edit it. From file in the table's source reads an .xlsx or .csv file instead.

Try next

  • Add a column to the table for the extrude's depth (right-click a cell in row 2 and pick the feature), give each size its own depth and show the three again.
  • Right-click Medium and choose Properties... to see the configuration's own properties.
  • Insert Block into an assembly: a list lets you choose which configuration to use, and the bill of materials shows its bare part name.

Tutorial 4: A small top-down assembly with a bill of materials

You will make a plate, build a lid inside the assembly so that it takes its shape from the plate, change the plate and watch the lid follow, and then put the assembly on a drawing with a bill of materials and balloons. It takes about 40 minutes. It uses chapter 5 (assemblies) and chapter 6 (drawings).

Part 1: the plate

  1. Press Ctrl+N, choose Part, click OK.
  2. Click Top Plane in the feature tree, then Sketch. Choose Corner Rectangle, click the origin and click up and to the right. Choose Smart Dimension and give the rectangle 100 by 60.
  3. Click Exit Sketch, then Extruded Boss/Base on the Features tab. Type 10 for the depth and press Enter to finish.
  4. Press Ctrl+S and save the part as Plate.
  5. Choose File Properties (File, Properties). On the Custom tab add a property named Description with the value Base plate, and click OK. The bill of materials reads this later.

Part 2: the assembly and the first part

  1. Press Ctrl+N and choose Assembly. The Begin Assembly panel opens on the left. The list shows the documents that are open.
  2. Click Plate in the list, then click in the graphics area. (Pressing OK, the green check, places it at the origin as well.) The first part in an assembly is fixed: its row in the tree starts with (f).
  3. Press Ctrl+S and save the assembly as Lid-Assembly.

Part 3: a new part made inside the assembly

  1. On the Assembly tab open the Insert Components flyout and choose New Part. The status bar says "Select a plane or face for the new part, or click in empty space to place it at the assembly origin."
  2. Click the top face of the plate. A new part appears in the tree with its name in square brackets, [Part1^Lid-Assembly]<1>. The brackets mean the part is kept inside the assembly file. An InPlace1 mate appears under Mates, and a sketch opens on the face you picked.
  3. While you edit the new part it is shown in blue, the other parts are see-through, the status bar says Editing Sketch1 while the lid's sketch is open and Editing Part after you exit the sketch, and the Edit Component button on the Assembly tab stays pressed.
  4. Click the top face of the plate, then choose Convert Entities on the Sketch tab. The four edges of the face come into the sketch at once as references to the plate.
  5. Click Exit Sketch, choose Extruded Boss/Base, type 5 and press Enter to finish. The lid is a thin cover with the same outline as the plate.
  6. Click Edit Component on the Assembly tab again to go back to the assembly. The colours return and the status bar says Editing Assembly. In the tree the lid's row ends in an arrow (->): it refers to another part, in context.

Part 4: the lid follows the plate

  1. Right-click the plate and click the Open Part icon in the row of icons at the top of the menu. Double-click the sketch of its base feature, change the 100 to 120 and click Rebuild on the top toolbar. Go back to the assembly with Window or the tab at the bottom, and click Rebuild (Ctrl+B) there too.
  2. After the assembly rebuilds, the lid is 120 long as well. Nothing was typed into the lid.
  3. Right-click the lid and choose External References.... There is one row for each feature that refers to the plate, with the status In context, the entity it follows (for example an edge of Plate<1>) and the part that owns the feature. Lock All stops the lid following, shown by ->\* in the tree; Unlock All lets it catch up again. Close the box with the lid following.
  4. Right-click the lid and choose Save Part (in External File). Save it as Lid. It keeps following the plate, but it is now a file of its own that a drawing and a bill of materials can use by name.

Part 5: names for the bill of materials

  1. The PART NUMBER column shows each part's document name (Plate, Lid). To use another number, open the part, open the ConfigurationManager tab, right-click the configuration, choose Properties..., and in Bill of Materials Options open the list Part number displayed when used in a bill of materials:, pick User Specified Name and type the number in the box under it.
  2. The DESCRIPTION column shows the part's Description custom property. You added one for the plate in step 5. Open Lid, open File Properties (the Summary Information box) and add a Description of Cover on the Custom tab.
  3. Save the parts and the assembly (Ctrl+S in each).

Part 6: the drawing

  1. With Lid-Assembly open press Ctrl+N and choose Drawing. Pick a sheet format, for example A3 (ISO).
  2. A new drawing opens the View Palette on the right, already set to Lid-Assembly. Drag the Isometric view (its name starts with a star) onto the sheet to place it.
  3. Click the view to select it. On the Annotation tab open the Tables flyout and choose Bill of Materials.
  4. In the panel leave Table Template on bom-standard (ITEM NO., PART NUMBER, DESCRIPTION, QTY.). Leave BOM Type on Top-level only. Click OK without clicking on the sheet: the table goes to the anchor point, the top right corner of the title block, and stays attached to it. (Attach to anchor point is off at first; the table is attached when you click OK without placing it.)
  5. Check the table: ITEM NO. 1 and 2, the part numbers Plate and Lid, the descriptions you typed and a QTY. of 1 each.

Part 7: balloons

  1. Click the view, then choose Auto Balloon on the Annotation tab. Leave the layout on Square (it comes from the Balloons page of Document Properties) and click OK. Each part gets a balloon with the same number as its row in the table.
  2. To place one yourself, choose Balloon, click a part in the view, then click where the balloon should go. The number is the part's ITEM NO. Click OK when you are done.
  3. Change the assembly (for example the plate's thickness), then go back to the drawing. The table updates by itself, and the balloons keep the item number of their part.

Try next

  • Add a third part inside the assembly with New Part on a side face of the plate, and watch the table gain a row.
  • Change Table Template to bom-material and see the MATERIAL column appear.
  • Use Indented in BOM Type on a larger assembly to list parts under their sub-assemblies.

Tutorial 5: Natural frequencies of a bar (frequency study)

You will fix one end of a steel bar, ask for its natural frequencies and look at the first mode shape. It takes about 15 minutes. It uses chapter 11. Tutorials 5, 6 and 7 all use the same bar, so make it once and save it as Bar.

Part 1: the bar

  1. Press Ctrl+N, choose Part, click OK.
  2. Click Right Plane, then Sketch. Choose Corner Rectangle and draw a square with one corner on the origin. Choose Smart Dimension and dimension both sides 10. The sketch turns black.
  3. Click Exit Sketch, then Extruded Boss/Base. Type 100 for the depth and press Enter to finish. The bar is 100 by 10 by 10 mm. Press Ctrl+S and save it as Bar.

Part 2: the study

  1. On the Simulation tab click New Study. Under General Simulation choose Frequency and click OK. The study is named Frequency 1. The study tree shows the part with its material (Plain Carbon Steel, which a new study applies), Connections, Fixtures, External Loads, Mesh and Results.
  2. Right-click Fixtures and choose Fixed Geometry.... Click the end face at the x = 0 end of the bar and click OK. Fixed-1 appears. Which end you fix does not matter, but the other tutorials call this one the x = 0 end.
  3. Optional: right-click the study name, choose Properties..., and set Number of frequencies (5 at first). Click OK.
  4. Optional: right-click Mesh, choose Create Mesh..., tick Mesh Parameters, choose Standard mesh, type a Global size of 4 mm and click OK. Without this, Run meshes the bar by itself and the numbers move by about 1 percent.

Part 3: run and read

  1. Click Run This Study. The pointer shows the wait cursor while the solver works. When it ends, Results holds Amplitude1 (-Res amp- Mode Shape 1-).
  2. Double-click Amplitude1. The legend shows the amplitude and the title reads "Mode Shape : 1 Value = ... Hz".
  3. Right-click Results and choose List Resonant Frequencies... for the list of modes, or List Mass Participation... (the last row has the sums for X, Y and Z).
  4. To see another mode, right-click Results, choose Define Mode Shape/Displacement Plot..., set the mode (for example 3) and click OK. You get Amplitude2 for that mode and the panel shows its frequency.

What you should get

  • With the 4 mm mesh, modes 1 and 2 are at about 835.9 Hz (the bar bends in two directions at the same frequency because the section is square) and modes 3 and 4 at about 5016.7 Hz. With the default mesh mode 1 is about 841 Hz.
  • A hand calculation for a bar fixed at one end, f = (1.875² / 2π) · √(E I / (ρ A L⁴)), gives 843.5 Hz.
  • The report has a mode list (frequency number, rad/s, hertz, seconds), the mass participation and the number of frequencies.

Try next

  • Make the bar 150 long and see the first frequency fall.
  • Fix both ends and compare the first mode.
  • Change the material and see how the frequency follows the square root of E over density.

Tutorial 6: When does a column buckle? (buckling study)

You will fix one end of the bar from tutorial 5, push on the other end and ask for the load at which it buckles. It takes about 15 minutes. It uses chapter 11.

  1. Open Bar (tutorial 5, Part 1). On the Simulation tab click New Study. Under Advanced Simulation choose Buckling and click OK. The study is named Buckling 1.
  2. Right-click Fixtures, choose Fixed Geometry..., click the end face at the x = 0 end and click OK.
  3. Right-click External Loads and choose Force.... Click the end face at the x = 100 end. Choose Selected direction, click in the Direction box, then click a long edge of the bar for the direction. Type 1000 N. If the arrow points away from the bar, tick Reverse direction so the force pushes into it. Click OK.
  4. Optional: right-click Mesh, choose Create Mesh..., tick Mesh Parameters, choose Standard mesh, type a Global size of 4 mm and click OK.
  5. Click Run This Study. The pointer shows the wait cursor while the solver works. When it ends the buckling mode shape is shown, and its title reads "Mode Shape : 1 Load Factor = ..." with a number of about 43 (43.046 with the 4 mm mesh). If the factor shows as -43 instead, the force pulls: the bar buckles only if the load is reversed, so tick Reverse direction so that it pushes.
  6. Right-click Results and choose List Buckling Factors.... The table has Mode 1 with a factor of about 43.

What you should get

  • The load factor is the number you multiply your load by to reach buckling. The bar buckles at about 43 × 1000 N, 43 kN.
  • A hand calculation for a column fixed at one end, P = π² E I / (4 L²), gives 43.2 kN, a load factor of 43.2 under 1000 N.
  • One buckling mode is asked for at first; the study's Properties set how many.
  • A load factor below 1 means the load you applied is already past buckling.

Try next

  • Make the bar 150 long: the load factor falls to a little under half.
  • Put a Roller/Slider fixture on the side faces near the loaded end and compare the load factor.
  • Apply 2000 N and notice that the load factor halves while the buckling load stays the same.

Tutorial 7: Heat along a bar (thermal study)

You will hold one end of the bar from tutorial 5 at 100 °C, cool the other end with air and read the temperatures along it. It takes about 15 minutes. It uses chapter 11.

  1. Open Bar (tutorial 5, Part 1). On the Simulation tab click New Study. Under Advanced Simulation choose Thermal and click OK. The study is named Thermal 1.
  2. The study tree shows Thermal Loads in place of Fixtures and External Loads. Right-click Thermal Loads to see Temperature..., Convection..., Heat Flux..., Heat Power... and Radiation....
  3. Choose Temperature.... Click the end face at the x = 0 end. Set the units to Celsius and the value to 100. Click OK. Temperature-1 appears.
  4. Right-click Thermal Loads again and choose Convection.... Click the end face at the x = 100 end. Set the Convection coefficient to 500 W/(m².K), the units to Celsius and the Bulk ambient temperature to 20. Click OK. Convection-1 appears.
  5. Optional: right-click Mesh, choose Create Mesh..., tick Mesh Parameters, choose Standard mesh, type a Global size of 4 mm and click OK.
  6. Click Run This Study. The pointer shows the wait cursor while the solver works.
  7. Under Results double-click Thermal1 (-Temperature-). The legend reads Temp (Kelvin).

What you should get

  • The hot end is at 373.15 K (100 °C).
  • The cooled end is at about 330.14 K (57 °C). It stays above the 293.15 K (20 °C) of the air, because the air only takes heat away through the end face as fast as the bar conducts it there.
  • A steady-state study has no initial temperature. The report lists the Thermal Loads with their names and values.

Try next

  • Replace the convection with a second Temperature of 25 °C. With both ends held, the middle of the bar is at the mean, 335.65 K.
  • Make the convection coefficient bigger and watch the cooled end fall towards 20 °C.
  • In the study's Properties... choose Transient with a total time of 60 s and a step of 20 s: the plots appear for Time = 20 s, 40 s and 60 s.
